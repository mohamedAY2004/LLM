Lesson 2 of 6 · Word2Vec (2013) · about 15 minutes

Meaning from the company a word keeps

Word2Vec is usually summed up as "meaning as geometry," with the famous king − man + woman ≈ queen. That summary leaves out the surprising part: nobody ever tells the model what a word means. The vectors are a by-product of a prediction task that nobody actually cares about.

1 · The fake task

Skip-gram slides a small window over raw text. For each center word, it tries to predict the words around it:

"I went to see the doctor about my cough"window ±2 → training pairs: (doctor, see), (doctor, the), (doctor, about), (doctor, my)

The model has two lookup tables, both filled with random numbers at the start:

  • E: one row per word, used when the word is the center. Call a row edoctor.
  • C: one row per word, used when the word is a neighbour. Call a row cpatient.

Training nudges both tables until the predictions improve. Then the prediction task is thrown away and E is kept. Those rows are the word vectors.Skip-gram and its mirror image CBOW (predict the center word from its neighbours) come from Mikolov et al. 2013a. The abstract reports learning "high quality word vectors from a 1.6 billion words data set" in under a day.

2 · Negative sampling: the version people actually train

Predicting "which word, out of the whole vocabulary, is the neighbour?" requires a softmax over tens of thousands of words at every step, which is expensive. Mikolov et al. 2013b replaced it with a much cheaper yes/no question:

P(real neighbours) = σ( ecenter · cword )σ is the logistic sigmoid, so this is logistic regression on a dot product. Push it toward 1 for real pairs and toward 0 for a few random "negative" words.

Every real pair (doctor, patient) comes with k fake pairs made by sampling random words: (doctor, banana), (doctor, volcano). The model learns to tell real neighbours from noise.

3 · The key idea: why "doctor" ends up next to "physician"

This is the step most summaries skip. "Doctor" and "physician" rarely appear in the same window, so no training step ever pulls them toward each other. But:

  • Every time "doctor" appears near "patient", edoctor moves toward cpatient.
  • Every time "physician" appears near "patient", ephysician moves toward the same cpatient.
  • The same happens for "hospital", "prescribed", "examined" and so on.

Two words pulled toward the same set of neighbour vectors end up near each other, even though they were never compared directly.

Remember this

J.R. Firth's 1957 line "you shall know a word by the company it keeps" is not a metaphor here. It is literally the gradient: shared neighbours → shared pull → nearby vectors. Word2Vec turns a linguistic hypothesis into an optimisation problem.

The analogy trick (king − man + woman ≈ queen) is a by-product of the same process. When many word pairs differ in the same way (man/woman, king/queen, uncle/aunt), that difference tends to show up as a roughly consistent direction in the space. The "≈" matters: the result is the nearest word to the computed point, not an exact hit.For what analogies really show and where they fail, see SLP3 §5.7 "Semantic properties of embeddings".

4 · What still breaks

Break 1 · the bank problem

E has one row per word type. "I sat on the river bank" and "the bank approved my loan" look up the same row. Its vector ends up a blend of both senses, weighted by how often each sense appears. This is a static embedding. It stays unsolved until lesson 6.

Break 2 · no sentence machinery

You now have good word vectors, but no way to read a sentence in order or to generate one. Averaging the vectors in "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" gives identical results. Translation needs both. That is lesson 3.

In 3–4 sentences, explain how Word2Vec makes "doctor" and "physician" similar without ever being told they're synonyms. Use the words neighbour, pull and shared.
