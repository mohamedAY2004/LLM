Lesson 3 of 6 · Seq2Seq (2014) · about 12 minutes

A whole sentence through a keyhole

Word2Vec gave us good word vectors but no way to read a sentence in order or to write one. Seq2Seq adds both by joining two RNNs. It works, but its shape has one built-in flaw. That flaw drives the next two lessons.

1 · The RNN in one line

This course assumes you've seen RNNs before, so here is only the part that matters:

ht = f( ht−1, xt )The hidden state h is a running summary with a fixed size. Each step folds one more word vector x into it. Step t can't start until step t−1 is done.

Keep two facts from that line: the summary never grows, and the computation is a chain.LSTMs and GRUs are gated versions of f that hold on to information longer. They don't change either fact. See SLP3 §14.5.

2 · The shape: encoder → context vector → decoder

A running example that will return in lesson 4: translate "the black cat sat" into Arabic, جلست القطة السوداء (word by word: sat · the-cat · the-black).

Encoder: an RNN reads the source word by word. Its final hidden state is the context vector c. Decoder: a second RNN starts from c and writes one word per step, feeding each word it writes back in as its next input, until it emits <EOS>. Input and output lengths no longer have to match. That is what "sequence to sequence" means.Sutskever, Vinyals & Le 2014 used multilayer LSTMs and reached 34.8 BLEU on English→French, beating a phrase-based statistical system.

Training detail you'll see in textbooks: during training, the decoder is fed the correct previous word rather than its own guess ("teacher forcing"). At inference time it has only its own guesses. See SLP3 §14.7.

3 · The bottleneck, and the evidence for it

Look at the size of c. It is fixed: often 256 to 1,024 numbersThe typical range quoted in Alammar's Seq2Seq visualisation., chosen before training. A 4-word sentence and a 60-word sentence both have to be squeezed into that same vector, and the decoder has no other view of the source. As a rough intuition, the room per source word shrinks as the sentence grows.

Evidence 1 · quality drops with length

Cho et al. (2014) studied exactly this. Encoder–decoder translation "performs relatively well on short sentences without unknown words, but its performance degrades rapidly as the length of the sentence and the number of unknown words increase."

Evidence 2 · the odd "reverse the input" trick

Sutskever et al. found that "reversing the order of the words in all source sentences (but not target sentences) improved the LSTM's performance markedly." Their explanation was that reversal "introduced many short term dependencies." If you feed sat cat black the, the first source word ends up right next to where the decoder starts. A trick that only changes distance helping this much tells you that information fades as it travels through the chain.

Now look at the Arabic. The decoder's first word, جلست (sat), corresponds to the last English word. Arabic is verb-first, so the alignment crosses over. Plain Seq2Seq has no way to "look at" sat directly. It has to dig the verb back out of c, which is a blend of everything. The reversal trick can't help both word orders at once.

What to take away

The bottleneck is a problem of shape, not of training effort. The encoder computes a useful state for every source word (h₁…h₄) and then the architecture throws all of them away except the last. The obvious question: why not let the decoder look at all of them?

4 · What still breaks

A single fixed-size c is the only path from source to target. That is the bottleneck. Lesson 4 keeps h₁…h₄ and lets the decoder choose, at every step, which of them to read from.

On paper, sketch Seq2Seq for "the black cat sat" → Arabic. Mark exactly where the bottleneck is, and write down two pieces of evidence that it hurts.
