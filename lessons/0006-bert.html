Lesson 6 of 6 · BERT (2018) · about 20 minutes

Reading in both directions at once

BERT adds almost no new architecture. It is the Transformer encoder from lesson 5. What's new is the training: a word-guessing game that makes the model use context from both sides of every word. That closes the loop with lesson 2, because "bank" finally gets a different vector in each sense.

1 · The recipe: pre-train, then fine-tune

Labelled data is scarce and raw text is plentiful. So first pre-train on raw text with a task where every word is its own label, which needs no annotators. Then fine-tune the same network on your small labelled task. BERT was pre-trained on BooksCorpus (800M words) plus English Wikipedia (2,500M words).Devlin et al. 2019, §3.1. The paper notes it is "critical to use a document-level corpus rather than a shuffled sentence-level corpus."

What makes BERT BERT is the choice of pre-training task.

2 · Why not just predict the next word?

Next-word prediction is what GPT (2018) did with a left-to-right Transformer decoder, where each word may only attend to words before it. That's natural for generating text, but it is a handicap for understanding. In "the ___ approved my loan," the clue is on the right.

So why not let every word attend to both sides and keep predicting words? Because the model would cheat. The BERT paper puts it this way: "bidirectional conditioning would allow each word to indirectly 'see itself', and the model could trivially predict the target word in a multi-layered context."

The fix · Masked Language Model (MLM)

Hide some input words by replacing them with [MASK], so they are gone from every position in every layer. Then predict them from both sides. BERT chooses 15% of the WordPiece tokens in each sequence and predicts only those.

3 · The 80 / 10 / 10 rule

This detail usually confuses people. Of the 15% of positions chosen for prediction:

  • 80% are replaced with [MASK]
  • 10% are replaced with a random token
  • 10% are left unchanged, and the model still has to predict them

Why not 100% [MASK]? Because [MASK] never appears when you fine-tune or use the model. The paper calls this "a mismatch between pre-training and fine-tuning." The deeper reason is in its appendix: the encoder "does not know which words it will be asked to predict or which have been replaced by random words, so it is forced to keep a distributional contextual representation of every input token." Any real-looking word might be a test, so the model has to represent all of them well. Random replacement touches only 1.5% of all tokens (10% of 15%), which is too little to disrupt what the model learns.Devlin et al., §3.1 and Appendix A.1.

The example sentence pair is from the BERT paper's appendix. Click Mask a new example a few times. Words without a "→ target" below them are not graded, so the model learns nothing directly from those positions in that example. That makes MLM less sample-efficient than next-word prediction, which grades every position. The paper accepts this trade-off.

4 · The input format

[CLS] the man went to the store [SEP] he bought a gallon of milk [SEP]

  • WordPiece tokens with a 30,000-token vocabulary. Rare words are split into pieces, and ## marks a piece that continues a word, as in "flight ##less."
  • [CLS] always comes first. Its final vector is "used as the aggregate sequence representation for classification tasks."
  • [SEP] separates sentence A from sentence B and ends the input.
  • Each token's input vector = token embedding + segment embedding (A or B) + position embedding. The position embedding is lesson 5's "add order back."

Next Sentence Prediction (NSP) was a second pre-training task. Half the time sentence B really follows A (IsNext), and half the time it is a random sentence (NotNext). The model predicts which from [CLS]. The BERT paper found that removing NSP hurt. RoBERTa later found that "removing the NSP loss matches or slightly improves downstream task performance," so treat NSP as a historical detail, not a key ingredient.Liu et al. 2019 (RoBERTa), §4.2. They also concluded BERT "was significantly undertrained."

6 · Fine-tuning: one small layer on top

To use BERT for a task, add one output layer and train all the weights a little on your labelled data:

  • Sentence classification (for example, sentiment): [CLS] review [SEP] → final [CLS] vector → linear layer + softmax → positive/negative.
  • Token tagging (named entities): a classifier on every token's final vector.
  • Question answering (SQuAD): [CLS] question [SEP] passage [SEP] → score each passage token as the answer's start or end.

Compare this with a classic TF-IDF + logistic regression classifier. In both cases a linear classifier sits on top. The difference is that the features under it are now contextual vectors from 12 layers of self-attention, instead of word counts.

7 · Closing the loop: contextual embeddings

The bank problem, solved, and the subtle part

BERT's input embedding for "bank" is still a single lookup-table row, as static as Word2Vec's. What changes is the output. After 12 layers of self-attention have mixed in "river" or "loan," the final vector for "bank" is different in each sentence. The context comes from the layers, not from the lookup table.

Evidence that both directions matter. The paper's ablation compares a left-to-right model with the masked model, both without NSP. Going left-to-right drops SQuAD F1 from 87.9 to 77.8 and MRPC from 86.5 to 77.5, while SST-2 sentiment barely moves (92.6 → 92.1). The paper explains the SQuAD result: "the token-level hidden states have no right-side context."Devlin et al., Table 5 and §5.1. The tasks that depend on each token understanding what follows it lose the most.

Where BERT sits today

The usual three-way taxonomy of Transformers now reads naturally. Encoder-only (BERT) is bidirectional, built for classification, tagging and embeddings. Decoder-only (GPT) is left-to-right, built for generation. Encoder–decoder (T5) is lesson 5's original shape. Decoder-only models went on to dominate generative AI, but BERT-style encoders are still widely used for search embeddings and classification, including language-specific versions such as AraBERT for Arabic.
