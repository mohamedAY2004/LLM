One failure at a time

Your decks treat TF-IDF, Word2Vec, RNNs, Transformers and BERT as separate topics. They are really one chain. Each step exists because the step before it broke in a specific way that you can name. Learn the chain first, and every later detail has a place to attach.

This lesson covers only the chain: five breaks and five fixes. Each of the next five lessons zooms into one link and works through the hard part the slides compress. Here, aim to be able to say "X broke because ___, so Y ___" for every step, from memory.

Where you already are

Decks 01–02 left you with TF-IDF: a document is a row of weighted word counts, and every vocabulary word is its own column. The decks already point to the two cracks this course follows:

  • No similarity between different words. "Doctor" vs "Physician" scores 0.00 (deck 02, slide 24). Each word is its own axis, at 90° to every other word.
  • No order. "Not bad, very good" and "Not good, very bad" get the same vector (deck 01, slide 18). It's a bag.

Those two cracks turn into two separate lines of work. Keep them apart in your head. That is the single most useful idea in this lesson.

The chain

Read each card as "what it does → what breaks." The orange line on each card is the reason the next card exists.

  1. TF-IDF / Bag of Wordsclassical

    A document is a vector of weighted word counts, one axis per vocabulary word. It works well for keyword search and linear classifiers (deck 02).

    Breaks: two documents are similar only if they share the exact same tokens, so doctor · physician = 0. Word order is lost.

  2. Word2Vec2013 · Mikolov et al.

    Each word gets a learned, dense vector (a few hundred numbers). The model learns them by predicting which words appear nearby, so words with similar neighbours end up close together.Original papers: Mikolov et al. 2013a (skip-gram, CBOW) and 2013b (negative sampling). Lesson 2 covers the training trick.

    Breaks: each word has one vector in every context ("river bank" = "bank loan"). It also provides nothing for reading a sentence in order or writing one out.

  3. Seq2Seq (encoder–decoder RNNs)2014 · Sutskever et al.; Cho et al.

    One RNN reads the input word by word and folds it into a hidden state. Its final state, the context vector, is passed to a second RNN that writes the output one word at a time. Order finally matters, and the input and output can be any length.

    Breaks: the whole sentence has to fit into one fixed-size vector (the bottleneck). Translation quality "degrades rapidly as the length of the sentence … increase[s]"Cho et al. 2014, abstract..

  4. Seq2Seq + attention2014/15 · Bahdanau, Cho, Bengio

    Keep every encoder hidden state. Before writing each output word, the decoder scores all of them, turns the scores into weights with softmax, and takes a weighted average. The result is a new context vector for every output word.

    Breaks: it still runs on RNNs, so word t can't be processed until word t−1 is done. Training can't run in parallel across the sentence (deck 03, slide 6).

  5. Transformer2017 · Vaswani et al.

    Remove the recurrence and keep only attention. Every word attends to every other word in the same sentence (self-attention), and all positions are computed at once.

    Breaks (for our story): it was trained on one labelled task (translation pairs), and most tasks lack that much labelled data. The obvious way to learn from raw text is next-word prediction, but that only lets each word see its left side.

  6. BERT2018 · Devlin et al.

    Use only the Transformer encoder. Pre-train it on 3.3 billion words of raw text by hiding 15% of the words and predicting them from both sides (masked LM), then fine-tune it on your task. Each word's output vector now depends on the whole sentence, so "bank" gets a different vector in each sense. That closes the break from card 2.

The one-sentence version

Word vectors got dense (Word2Vec), sentences got ordered (Seq2Seq), the decoder learned to look back (attention), the RNN was dropped (Transformer), and the encoder learned from raw text in both directions (BERT).
